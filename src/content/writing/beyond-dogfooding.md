I've always believed in dogfooding: build the product, use it yourself, live with its rough edges.

Dogfooding worked because the builder and the user were the same kind of creature. A human building for humans feels friction directly. You immediately notice when something technically works yet makes no sense.

That breaks down when the user is an AI agent. I can't use a product the way an agent does. I can build an agent, point it at my API, watch its traces, and even ask it afterward where it got stuck. None of that is the same as using the product myself. I'm observing a foreign entity from the outside.

An agent only sees what your interface and documentation give it. Nobody is sitting next to it to explain unwritten conventions or legacy workarounds. The test is whether the system explains itself clearly on its own.

Other fields have dealt with this for decades. Clinical researchers, anthropologists, and accessibility designers routinely build for users they cannot be. They can't rely on personal intuition; they depend on structured observation and method. They move deliberately. Most engineering organizations are slow too, just without the method.

When what sits on the other side of the API is a different species, we stop being users of our own products and become researchers of them. We need a discipline better than dogfooding, and most teams haven't built it yet.
