For much of my career, I was cursed by being too early.

Rules-based website personalization in 1997, when static pages were still the norm. Mobile marketing SaaS in 2000, when the available channel was plain SMS. Mobile games in 2006, before app stores or modern smartphones existed. In each case, the underlying thesis was right, and the timing was off by years.

When building a serious software product took two to four years and substantial capital, being early was indistinguishable from being wrong. You burned runway waiting for infrastructure and customer habits to catch up, and later entrants captured the market once conditions matured.

AI changes the economics of that timing penalty by compressing the iteration cycle. Systems that used to require multi-year engineering commitments can now be prototyped, put in front of real users, and adjusted in months. You can test a thesis cheaply, learn where reality pushes back, and pivot before capital or conviction runs out.

There's an old engineering joke that you can only pick two out of fast, cheap, and good. AI hands almost everyone fast and cheap by default. The remaining differentiator is judgment: making the product genuinely good and pointing it at a real problem.

That also moves the advantage away from pure tooling fluency toward deep domain understanding. Knowing how an industry actually breaks, where workflows stall, and what customers will pay to fix matters more than being the fastest prompt engineer in the room.

When the cost of testing an idea drops by an order of magnitude, seeing around the corner stops being a financial liability and becomes a real advantage.
