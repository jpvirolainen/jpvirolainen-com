Suppose one engineering team gets three times faster as AI starts writing more of its code. If the deliverable still depends on three other teams, how much faster did the organization actually get?

In most large companies, different teams are at very different stages of AI adoption. The assumptions people used to rely on about how long another team needs to deliver become even less reliable. Local speed gains don't automatically translate into end-to-end delivery; they just show up as waiting.

Planning across teams has always been hard, and AI is making the friction more obvious. Where cross-team dependencies still exist, you need clearer synchronization, not looser coordination.

In an ideal setup, work flows continuously without ceremonial planning windows. Across large multi-team initiatives, that's rarely the reality. I have no ideological attachment to sprints, which carry plenty of flaws, but when dependent teams run on the same cadence, planning and commitments at least happen at the same time.

Shared planning also does something more useful: it forces dependencies into the open and makes it obvious which ones you should eliminate through architecture and team boundaries.

That leaves an odd temporary paradox: many organizations need tighter synchronization today so they can see and remove the coupling that lets them need less synchronization tomorrow.
